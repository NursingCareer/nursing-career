🦴 Chapter 01 — Skeletal System

1. Definition

The skeletal system is the framework of the human body formed mainly by bones and cartilage. It provides support, protection, movement, mineral storage and blood-cell formation.

2. Major Functions of Bones

  • Support the body.
  • Protect internal organs.
  • Help movement by providing attachment for muscles.
  • Store calcium and phosphate.
  • Red bone marrow produces blood cells.
  • Fat is stored in yellow bone marrow.

3. Divisions of Skeleton

Division Main Components
Axial skeleton Skull, vertebral column, ribs, sternum
Appendicular skeleton Shoulder girdle, upper limbs, pelvic girdle, lower limbs

An adult human skeleton normally contains 206 bones.

4. Skull

The skull forms the bony framework of the head. It protects the brain and forms the cavities for special sense organs.

Cranial bones

  • Frontal
  • Parietal — 2
  • Temporal — 2
  • Occipital
  • Sphenoid
  • Ethmoid

Facial bones

  • Maxillae
  • Mandible
  • Zygomatic bones
  • Nasal bones
  • Lacrimal bones
  • Palatine bones
  • Vomer
  • Inferior nasal conchae
✍️ Hand Note: Mandible is the movable bone of the skull. The cranium protects the brain.

5. Vertebral Column

The vertebral column forms the central supporting axis of the body. It protects the spinal cord and provides attachment for ribs and muscles.

Region Number
Cervical 7
Thoracic 12
Lumbar 5
Sacral 5 fused
Coccygeal Usually 4 fused

Total vertebrae are commonly described as 33 before fusion. The adult vertebral column has 26 bones because sacral and coccygeal vertebrae fuse.

6. Thoracic Cage

The thoracic cage protects the heart and lungs and assists respiration. It consists of sternum, thoracic vertebrae and ribs.

Ribs

  • True ribs: 1–7
  • False ribs: 8–10
  • Floating ribs: 11–12

7. Upper Limb Bones

  • Clavicle
  • Scapula
  • Humerus
  • Radius
  • Ulna
  • Carpal bones
  • Metacarpals
  • Phalanges

The radius is located on the thumb side of the forearm. The ulna is on the little-finger side.

8. Lower Limb Bones

  • Hip bone
  • Femur
  • Patella
  • Tibia
  • Fibula
  • Tarsals
  • Metatarsals
  • Phalanges
🔥 High Yield: Femur is the longest and strongest bone of the human body. Patella is the largest sesamoid bone.

9. Structure of a Long Bone

  • Epiphysis — ends of bone
  • Diaphysis — shaft
  • Metaphysis — region between shaft and epiphysis
  • Periosteum — outer covering
  • Endosteum — inner lining
  • Compact bone
  • Spongy/cancellous bone
  • Medullary cavity

10. Bone Marrow

Red bone marrow is involved in hematopoiesis. Yellow bone marrow mainly contains adipose tissue and serves as fat storage.

11. Joints

A joint is the site where two or more bones meet.

Major types

  • Fibrous joints
  • Cartilaginous joints
  • Synovial joints

Examples of synovial joints

  • Shoulder — ball and socket
  • Hip — ball and socket
  • Elbow — hinge
  • Knee — modified hinge
  • Wrist — condyloid
  • Atlanto-axial — pivot

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Fracture means break in bone continuity.
  • Osteoporosis causes reduced bone density.
  • Osteomyelitis is infection of bone.
  • Dislocation means loss of normal joint relationship.
  • Arthritis affects joints.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Positioning of fracture patients.
  • Assessment of limb circulation.
  • Checking distal pulse after injury.
  • Monitoring swelling and pain.
  • Preventing pressure injury in immobile patients.

✍️ Hand Note

206 bones → adult skeleton
7 cervical → 12 thoracic → 5 lumbar
True ribs 1–7
Floating ribs 11–12
Femur = longest bone
Patella = largest sesamoid bone
Red marrow = blood-cell formation

💪 Chapter 02 — Muscular System

1. Definition

The muscular system consists of specialized tissues capable of contraction. Muscles produce movement, maintain posture and generate heat.

2. Types of Muscle

Type Location Control
Skeletal Attached to bones Voluntary
Smooth Viscera and blood vessels Involuntary
Cardiac Heart Involuntary

3. Skeletal Muscle Structure

A skeletal muscle is made of muscle fibers. Fibers contain myofibrils, and myofibrils contain actin and myosin filaments.

Important terms

  • Muscle fiber
  • Myofibril
  • Actin
  • Myosin
  • Sarcomere
  • Tendon
  • Fascia

4. Major Muscles of Head and Neck

  • Masseter — mastication
  • Temporalis — mastication
  • Sternocleidomastoid — movement of head and neck
  • Trapezius — movement of neck and shoulder

5. Thoracic Muscles

  • Intercostal muscles
  • Pectoralis major
  • Pectoralis minor
  • Diaphragm

The diaphragm is the principal muscle of inspiration.

6. Abdominal Muscles

  • Rectus abdominis
  • External oblique
  • Internal oblique
  • Transversus abdominis

7. Upper Limb Muscles

  • Deltoid
  • Biceps brachii
  • Triceps brachii
  • Forearm flexors
  • Forearm extensors

Biceps mainly flexes the elbow, while triceps extends the elbow.

8. Lower Limb Muscles

  • Gluteus maximus
  • Quadriceps femoris
  • Hamstrings
  • Gastrocnemius
  • Soleus

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Muscle strain may occur due to overstretching.
  • Muscle atrophy occurs when muscle mass decreases.
  • Paralysis causes loss of voluntary movement.
  • Muscle spasm causes involuntary contraction.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Assess muscle strength.
  • Observe mobility.
  • Prevent contractures in immobile patients.
  • Encourage appropriate range-of-motion exercises.

✍️ Hand Note

Skeletal = voluntary
Smooth = involuntary
Cardiac = heart
Diaphragm = main muscle of inspiration
Biceps = flexion
Triceps = extension

Q: Which muscle is found in the heart?
Ans: Cardiac muscle.

❤️ Chapter 03 — Cardiovascular System

1. Overview

The cardiovascular system consists of the heart, blood and blood vessels. Its major role is transportation of oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste products.

2. Location of Heart

The heart lies in the thoracic cavity within the mediastinum, between the two lungs. It is slightly left of the midline.

3. Coverings of Heart

  • Pericardium
  • Fibrous pericardium
  • Serous pericardium

The pericardial cavity contains a small amount of lubricating fluid.

4. Chambers of Heart

Chamber Main Role
Right atrium Receives deoxygenated blood from body
Right ventricle Pumps blood to lungs
Left atrium Receives oxygenated blood from lungs
Left ventricle Pumps blood to systemic circulation

5. Heart Valves

  • Tricuspid valve — right atrioventricular valve
  • Mitral/bicuspid valve — left atrioventricular valve
  • Pulmonary valve
  • Aortic valve
🔥 High Yield: Tricuspid = right side. Mitral = left side.

6. Great Vessels

  • Superior vena cava
  • Inferior vena cava
  • Pulmonary trunk
  • Pulmonary veins
  • Aorta

7. Coronary Circulation

The myocardium receives blood mainly from the right and left coronary arteries. Cardiac veins drain into the coronary sinus and then into the right atrium.

8. Blood Vessels

Arteries

Carry blood away from the heart.

Veins

Carry blood toward the heart.

Capillaries

Small vessels where exchange of gases, nutrients and wastes occurs.

9. Pulmonary and Systemic Circulation

Pulmonary circulation: Right ventricle → pulmonary arteries → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium.

Systemic circulation: Left ventricle → aorta → body tissues → venae cavae → right atrium.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Myocardial infarction involves injury to heart muscle due to inadequate blood supply.
  • Hypertension means persistently elevated arterial blood pressure.
  • Heart failure occurs when the heart cannot adequately meet body demands.
  • Arrhythmia refers to abnormal cardiac rhythm.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Points

  • Check pulse rate and rhythm.
  • Monitor blood pressure.
  • Observe chest pain and dyspnea.
  • Assess peripheral perfusion.
  • Monitor edema.

✍️ Hand Note

Heart = 4 chambers
Right heart → deoxygenated blood
Left heart → oxygenated blood
Tricuspid → right AV valve
Mitral → left AV valve
Aorta → largest artery
Pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood

❓ Short Questions

Q: How many chambers does the heart have?
Ans: Four.
Q: Which chamber pumps blood to systemic circulation?
Ans: Left ventricle.

🫁 Chapter 04 — Respiratory System

1. Definition

The respiratory system is responsible for ventilation and exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide between the atmosphere and blood.

2. Respiratory Tract

  1. Nose
  2. Nasal cavity
  3. Pharynx
  4. Larynx
  5. Trachea
  6. Bronchi
  7. Bronchioles
  8. Alveoli
  9. Lungs

3. Nose and Nasal Cavity

The nose provides an entrance for air. Nasal structures help filter, warm and humidify inspired air.

4. Pharynx

The pharynx is divided into:

  • Nasopharynx
  • Oropharynx
  • Laryngopharynx

5. Larynx

The larynx connects the pharynx with the trachea and contains the vocal cords. The epiglottis helps prevent food entering the airway during swallowing.

6. Trachea and Bronchi

The trachea divides into right and left main bronchi. These continue into smaller bronchi, bronchioles and finally respiratory passages.

7. Lungs

Lung Lobes
Right lung Superior, middle, inferior
Left lung Superior, inferior

The left lung has a cardiac notch to accommodate the heart.

8. Pleura

  • Visceral pleura covers the lung.
  • Parietal pleura lines the thoracic cavity.
  • Pleural cavity lies between them.

9. Alveoli

Alveoli are tiny air sacs where the major exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide occurs between air and pulmonary capillary blood.

10. Respiratory Muscles

  • Diaphragm
  • External intercostal muscles
  • Internal intercostal muscles
  • Accessory muscles during increased respiratory effort

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Pneumonia affects lung tissue.
  • Asthma causes reversible airway narrowing and inflammation.
  • Pneumothorax is air in the pleural space.
  • Pleural effusion is excess fluid in pleural space.
  • Respiratory failure can impair oxygenation and/or carbon dioxide removal.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Assess respiratory rate.
  • Observe chest movement.
  • Check oxygen saturation.
  • Assess breath sounds.
  • Position patients appropriately to improve ventilation.

✍️ Hand Note

Right lung = 3 lobes
Left lung = 2 lobes
Alveoli = gas exchange
Pleura = lung covering
Diaphragm = principal inspiratory muscle
Epiglottis = protects airway during swallowing

📝 MCQ

1. Main site of gas exchange:
A. Trachea
B. Bronchus
C. Alveoli
D. Larynx
Answer: C. Alveoli
2. Right lung has:
A. 1 lobe
B. 2 lobes
C. 3 lobes
D. 4 lobes
Answer: C. 3 lobes

🧠 Chapter 05 — Nervous System

1. Overview

The nervous system controls and coordinates body activities and enables the body to respond to internal and external stimuli.

2. Divisions

  • Central nervous system — brain and spinal cord
  • Peripheral nervous system — cranial and spinal nerves

The peripheral nervous system includes sensory and motor components. The autonomic nervous system regulates many involuntary functions.

3. Brain

The brain is located within the cranial cavity and is protected by the skull, meninges and cerebrospinal fluid.

Major parts

  • Cerebrum
  • Diencephalon
  • Brainstem
  • Cerebellum

4. Cerebrum

The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain and is divided into right and left cerebral hemispheres.

Major lobes

  • Frontal lobe
  • Parietal lobe
  • Temporal lobe
  • Occipital lobe
🔥 High Yield:
Frontal → motor functions, planning and aspects of speech
Parietal → somatic sensory processing
Temporal → hearing and memory-related functions
Occipital → vision

5. Cerebellum

The cerebellum contributes to coordination of movement, balance and maintenance of posture.

6. Brainstem

The brainstem consists of midbrain, pons and medulla oblongata. It contains important pathways and centers involved in vital functions.

7. Spinal Cord

The spinal cord extends from the brainstem through the vertebral canal. It carries nerve impulses between the brain and peripheral structures and participates in reflex activity.

8. Meninges

  1. Dura mater
  2. Arachnoid mater
  3. Pia mater

9. Cerebrospinal Fluid

CSF surrounds the brain and spinal cord. It provides cushioning and helps maintain the environment of the central nervous system.

10. Autonomic Nervous System

  • Sympathetic division
  • Parasympathetic division

These systems regulate functions such as heart rate, digestion, pupil diameter and glandular activity.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Stroke results from interruption or bleeding involving brain blood supply.
  • Meningitis affects the meninges.
  • Epilepsy involves recurrent abnormal electrical activity in the brain.
  • Spinal cord injury can cause sensory and motor deficits.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Assess level of consciousness.
  • Check pupil reaction.
  • Assess motor and sensory function.
  • Monitor vital signs.
  • Observe signs of increased intracranial pressure.

✍️ Hand Note

CNS = Brain + spinal cord
PNS = cranial + spinal nerves
Cerebellum = coordination and balance
Occipital lobe = vision
Temporal lobe = hearing
Meninges = dura, arachnoid, pia

📝 MCQ

1. CNS consists of:
A. Brain and spinal cord

C. Spinal nerves only
D. Cranial nerves only
Answer: A. Brain and spinal cord
2. Cerebellum is mainly associated with:
A. Vision
B. Balance and coordination

🍽️ Chapter 06 — Digestive System

1. Overview

The digestive system breaks food into smaller substances that can be absorbed and used by the body.

2. Alimentary Canal

  1. Mouth
  2. Pharynx
  3. Esophagus
  4. Stomach
  5. Small intestine
  6. Large intestine
  7. Rectum
  8. Anal canal

3. Mouth

Digestion begins in the mouth. Teeth mechanically break food and saliva helps moisten food and begins digestion of carbohydrates.

Major salivary glands

  • Parotid
  • Submandibular
  • Sublingual

4. Esophagus

The esophagus is a muscular tube carrying swallowed food from the pharynx to the stomach through coordinated peristaltic contractions.

5. Stomach

The stomach is a muscular hollow organ located mainly in the upper left part of the abdomen.

Parts

  • Cardia
  • Fundus
  • Body
  • Pyloric part

The stomach stores food, mixes it and begins important aspects of protein digestion.

6. Small Intestine

  • Duodenum
  • Jejunum
  • Ileum

Most digestion and nutrient absorption occurs in the small intestine. Villi and microvilli increase absorptive surface area.

7. Large Intestine

  • Cecum
  • Appendix
  • Ascending colon
  • Transverse colon
  • Descending colon
  • Sigmoid colon
  • Rectum
  • Anal canal

The large intestine mainly absorbs water and electrolytes and forms feces.

8. Liver

The liver is the largest internal gland and is located mainly in the right upper abdomen.

Major functions

  • Produces bile.
  • Processes nutrients.
  • Stores glycogen and certain vitamins/minerals.
  • Detoxification and metabolism of many substances.
  • Synthesis of many plasma proteins.

9. Gallbladder

The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver.

10. Pancreas

The pancreas has both exocrine and endocrine functions. Its exocrine part produces digestive enzymes and bicarbonate. Its endocrine part produces hormones such as insulin and glucagon.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Appendicitis affects the appendix.
  • Gastritis involves inflammation of gastric mucosa.
  • Peptic ulcer disease can affect stomach or duodenum.
  • Hepatitis affects the liver.
  • Gallstones can obstruct the biliary system.
  • Pancreatitis involves inflammation of the pancreas.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Assess abdominal pain.
  • Monitor bowel pattern.
  • Observe vomiting and dehydration.
  • Monitor nutritional status.
  • Assess stool characteristics when clinically indicated.

✍️ Hand Note

Small intestine = duodenum + jejunum + ileum
Liver = produces bile
Gallbladder = stores bile
Pancreas = digestive enzymes + hormones
Large intestine = water absorption

Answer: B. Small intestine

🚽 Chapter 07 — Urinary System

1. Components

  • Two kidneys
  • Two ureters
  • Urinary bladder
  • Urethra

2. Kidney

The kidneys are paired organs located posteriorly in the abdomen. They regulate fluid and electrolyte balance and remove metabolic wastes through urine formation.

3. External Structure

  • Superior pole
  • Inferior pole
  • Anterior surface
  • Posterior surface
  • Medial border
  • Lateral border
  • Renal hilum

4. Internal Structure

  • Renal cortex
  • Renal medulla
  • Renal pyramids
  • Renal papillae
  • Minor calyces
  • Major calyces
  • Renal pelvis

5. Nephron

The nephron is the functional unit of the kidney.

  • Renal corpuscle
  • Glomerulus
  • Bowman's capsule
  • Proximal convoluted tubule
  • Loop of Henle
  • Distal convoluted tubule
  • Collecting duct

6. Functions of Kidney

  • Urine formation.
  • Removal of metabolic waste.
  • Regulation of water balance.
  • Regulation of electrolytes.
  • Contribution to acid-base balance.
  • Renin production.
  • Erythropoietin production.
  • Activation of vitamin D.

7. Ureter

Each ureter carries urine from the renal pelvis to the urinary bladder. Peristaltic movement helps transport urine.

8. Urinary Bladder

The urinary bladder is a muscular reservoir that temporarily stores urine. Its muscular wall contains the detrusor muscle.

9. Urethra

The urethra carries urine from the bladder to the outside of the body. Its length and anatomical relationships differ between males and females.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Urinary tract infection can involve bladder, urethra, ureters or kidneys.
  • Renal calculi are kidney stones.
  • Acute kidney injury causes sudden reduction in kidney function.
  • Chronic kidney disease causes progressive loss of kidney function.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Monitor urine output.
  • Observe urine color and characteristics.
  • Maintain catheter care when indicated.
  • Monitor fluid balance.
  • Observe signs of urinary infection.

✍️ Hand Note

Functional unit = nephron
Kidney → ureter → bladder → urethra
Glomerulus = filtration site
Bladder = urine storage

🧪 Chapter 08 — Endocrine System

1. Definition

The endocrine system consists of glands that release hormones into the bloodstream. Hormones regulate metabolism, growth, reproduction, stress responses and many other physiological functions.

2. Major Endocrine Glands

  • Hypothalamus
  • Pituitary gland
  • Thyroid gland
  • Parathyroid glands
  • Adrenal glands
  • Pancreatic islets
  • Pineal gland
  • Thymus
  • Ovaries
  • Testes

3. Pituitary Gland

The pituitary is located at the base of the brain in the sella turcica. It is closely connected with the hypothalamus.

It has anterior and posterior components and participates in regulation of growth, thyroid function, adrenal function, reproduction and water balance.

4. Thyroid Gland

The thyroid lies in the anterior neck around the trachea. It produces thyroid hormones that influence metabolic activity.

5. Parathyroid Glands

Usually four small glands are located on the posterior surface of the thyroid. Parathyroid hormone plays a major role in calcium regulation.

6. Adrenal Glands

The adrenal glands sit superior to the kidneys.

  • Cortex
  • Medulla

The cortex produces steroid hormones including glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids. The medulla produces catecholamines.

7. Pancreatic Islets

The endocrine pancreas contains islets of Langerhans. Insulin and glucagon are important hormones involved in blood glucose regulation.

8. Pineal and Thymus

The pineal gland is involved in circadian rhythm regulation through melatonin secretion.

The thymus is important in development of T lymphocytes and is most active during childhood.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Diabetes mellitus involves abnormal glucose regulation.
  • Hypothyroidism results from insufficient thyroid hormone effect.
  • Hyperthyroidism involves excessive thyroid hormone effect.
  • Adrenal disorders can alter cortisol or aldosterone-related functions.

✍️ Hand Note

Pituitary → major regulatory endocrine gland
Thyroid → metabolism
Parathyroid → calcium regulation
Adrenal → stress and fluid/electrolyte-related hormones
Pancreas → insulin + glucagon
Pineal → melatonin

♀️ Chapter 09 — Female Reproductive System

1. Main Components

  • Ovaries
  • Uterine tubes
  • Uterus
  • Cervix
  • Vagina
  • External genitalia
  • Mammary glands

2. Ovary

The ovaries are paired female gonads located in the pelvis. They produce ova and secrete reproductive hormones including estrogen and progesterone.

3. Uterine Tube

The uterine tube extends from the uterus toward the ovary. It is divided into:

  • Infundibulum
  • Ampulla
  • Isthmus
  • Uterine part

The ampulla is the usual site of fertilization.

4. Uterus

The uterus is a muscular hollow organ located in the pelvis. It provides the site for implantation and development of pregnancy.

Parts

  • Fundus
  • Body
  • Isthmus
  • Cervix

Layers

  • Perimetrium
  • Myometrium
  • Endometrium

5. Cervix

The cervix is the lower narrow part of the uterus that projects into the upper vagina. It has important roles during menstruation, pregnancy and childbirth.

6. Vagina

The vagina is a muscular canal extending from the cervix to the external genital tract.

7. External Genitalia

The external female genitalia are collectively called the vulva and include structures such as the labia and clitoris.

8. Mammary Glands

Mammary glands are specialized structures associated with lactation. They contain glandular tissue, ducts and supporting connective tissue.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Ectopic pregnancy commonly involves implantation outside the uterine cavity, often in a uterine tube.
  • Uterine fibroids are benign tumors of uterine smooth muscle.
  • Endometriosis involves endometrial-like tissue outside the uterine cavity.
  • Cervical disease may be detected through screening.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Understand pelvic anatomy for obstetric assessment.
  • Know uterine layers for understanding contractions and postpartum changes.
  • Understand reproductive anatomy for patient education.

✍️ Hand Note

Ovary → ova + hormones
Uterine tube → transport of ovum
Ampulla → common site of fertilization
Uterus → implantation and pregnancy
Myometrium → uterine muscle
Endometrium → inner uterine lining

♂️ Chapter 10 — Male Reproductive System

1. Main Components

  • Testes
  • Epididymis
  • Vas deferens
  • Seminal vesicles
  • Prostate gland
  • Bulbourethral glands
  • Penis
  • Scrotum

2. Testes

The testes are male gonads located in the scrotum. They produce sperm and testosterone.

Inside the testes are seminiferous tubules where sperm production occurs.

3. Epididymis

The epididymis is a coiled tube attached to the posterior aspect of the testis. Sperm mature and are stored here before transport.

4. Vas Deferens

The vas deferens transports sperm from the epididymis toward the ejaculatory ducts.

5. Seminal Vesicles

Seminal vesicles contribute fluid containing substances that support sperm function and form part of semen.

6. Prostate Gland

The prostate is located inferior to the urinary bladder and surrounds part of the urethra. It contributes fluid to semen.

7. Penis

The penis is the male copulatory organ and also provides a passage for urine through the urethra.

8. Scrotum

The scrotum contains the testes and helps maintain a temperature favorable for sperm production.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Benign prostatic enlargement can affect urinary flow.
  • Prostatitis is inflammation of the prostate.
  • Testicular torsion can compromise testicular blood supply.
  • Undescended testis is called cryptorchidism.

✍️ Hand Note

Testis → sperm + testosterone
Epididymis → sperm maturation
Vas deferens → sperm transport
Prostate → contributes to semen
Scrotum → temperature regulation

🛡️ Chapter 11 — Lymphatic System

1. Overview

The lymphatic system participates in fluid balance, absorption of certain dietary fats and immune defense.

2. Major Components

  • Lymph
  • Lymphatic vessels
  • Lymph nodes
  • Spleen
  • Thymus
  • Tonsils

3. Lymph

Lymph is fluid that enters lymphatic vessels from tissue spaces. It eventually returns to the venous circulation.

4. Lymph Nodes

Lymph nodes are small lymphoid organs distributed along lymphatic vessels. They filter lymph and provide sites for immune-cell activity.

5. Spleen

The spleen is located in the left upper abdomen. It filters blood, participates in immune responses and removes aged blood cells.

6. Thymus

The thymus is located in the anterior mediastinum and is important for T-lymphocyte development.

7. Major Lymphatic Ducts

  • Thoracic duct
  • Right lymphatic duct

The thoracic duct drains lymph from most of the body. The right lymphatic duct drains the right upper quadrant.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Lymphadenopathy means enlarged lymph nodes.
  • Lymphedema occurs when lymph drainage is impaired.
  • Lymphatic spread can be important in cancer staging.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Assess enlarged lymph nodes when indicated.
  • Observe limb swelling.
  • Understand lymphatic drainage after surgery.
  • Provide appropriate care for lymphedema according to clinical guidance.

✍️ Hand Note

Spleen → filters blood
Thymus → T-cell development
Lymph nodes → filter lymph
Thoracic duct → drains most of body

👁️ Chapter 12 — Special Senses

1. Special Senses

  • Vision
  • Hearing
  • Balance
  • Smell
  • Taste

2. Eye

The eye is the organ of vision and is located within the orbit.

Major structures

  • Cornea
  • Sclera
  • Choroid
  • Iris
  • Pupil
  • Lens
  • Retina
  • Optic nerve

3. Cornea

The cornea is the transparent anterior part of the eye and contributes significantly to focusing light.

4. Iris and Pupil

The iris controls the diameter of the pupil and therefore regulates the amount of light entering the eye.

5. Lens

The lens changes shape to help focus light on the retina.

6. Retina

The retina contains photoreceptor cells that convert light stimuli into neural signals.

7. Ear

External ear

  • Auricle
  • External acoustic canal

Middle ear

  • Tympanic membrane
  • Malleus
  • Incus
  • Stapes

Inner ear

  • Cochlea
  • Vestibule
  • Semicircular canals

8. Hearing and Balance

The cochlea is mainly associated with hearing. The vestibular apparatus contributes to balance and spatial orientation.

9. Smell and Taste

Olfactory receptors detect smell. Taste receptors are located mainly within taste buds of the tongue.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Cataract affects the lens and causes visual impairment.
  • Glaucoma is associated with optic nerve damage and abnormal intraocular pressure in many cases.
  • Otitis media affects the middle ear.
  • Hearing loss can result from problems in conductive or sensorineural pathways.

🧴 Chapter 13 — Integumentary System

1. Overview

The integumentary system includes skin and associated structures such as hair, nails and cutaneous glands. It provides protection and helps regulate body temperature and sensation.

2. Layers of Skin

  • Epidermis
  • Dermis
  • Subcutaneous tissue

3. Epidermis

The epidermis is the outer epithelial layer of skin. It provides a protective barrier and contains keratinocytes and other specialized cells.

4. Dermis

The dermis lies beneath the epidermis and contains connective tissue, blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles and glands.

5. Subcutaneous Tissue

Subcutaneous tissue lies below the dermis and contains connective and adipose tissue. It helps with insulation, energy storage and cushioning.

6. Skin Appendages

  • Hair
  • Nails
  • Sweat glands
  • Sebaceous glands

7. Functions of Skin

  • Protection
  • Temperature regulation
  • Sensation
  • Prevention of excessive water loss
  • Immune defense
  • Vitamin D-related processes

8. Sweat Glands

Sweat glands contribute to thermoregulation by producing sweat. Evaporation of sweat helps remove heat from the body.

9. Sebaceous Glands

Sebaceous glands produce sebum, an oily secretion that helps lubricate skin and hair.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Burns damage skin and may affect deeper tissues.
  • Pressure injuries develop when prolonged pressure compromises tissue perfusion.
  • Dermatitis causes inflammation of skin.
  • Wounds involve disruption of tissue integrity.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Inspect skin regularly.
  • Assess pressure points in immobile patients.
  • Maintain skin hygiene.
  • Assess wounds and burns.
  • Prevent pressure injuries through repositioning and appropriate support surfaces.

✍️ Hand Note

Skin layers → epidermis + dermis + subcutaneous tissue
Epidermis → outer layer
Dermis → vessels, nerves, glands, follicles
Sweat → temperature regulation
Sebum → lubrication
