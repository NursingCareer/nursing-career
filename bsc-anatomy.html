🦴 Chapter 01 — Skeletal System

Definition

The skeletal system is the supporting framework of the human body. It is composed mainly of bones and cartilage. Bones provide support, protect important organs, provide attachment for muscles and participate in movement, mineral storage and blood-cell formation.

🖼️ Human Skeleton — Major Bones

Major bones of the human skeleton — simplified educational diagram

Major Functions

  • Support and shape the body.
  • Protect vital organs.
  • Assist movement.
  • Store calcium and phosphate.
  • Bone marrow contributes to blood-cell formation.
  • Yellow marrow stores fat.

Divisions of Skeleton

Division Components
Axial skeleton Skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum
Appendicular skeleton Upper limbs, lower limbs and their girdles

An adult human skeleton normally contains 206 bones.

Skull

The skull forms the bony framework of the head and protects the brain. It also forms cavities for the eyes, nose, mouth and ears.

Cranial bones

  • Occipital

Thoracic Cage

The thoracic cage protects the heart and lungs and participates in respiratory movements.

Joints

  • Fibrous joint

💪 Chapter 02 — Muscular System

Definition

Muscle is specialized tissue capable of contraction. Muscle contraction produces movement, maintains posture, supports joints and generates heat.

🖼️ Three Types of Muscle

Skeletal = voluntary • Smooth = involuntary • Cardiac = heart

Muscle Control Location
Skeletal Voluntary Attached to bones
Smooth Involuntary Viscera and vessels
Cardiac Involuntary Heart

Major Muscles

  • Masseter — mastication
  • Sternocleidomastoid — head and neck movement
  • Trapezius — shoulder movement
  • Deltoid — shoulder abduction
  • Biceps — elbow flexion
  • Triceps — elbow extension
  • Quadriceps — knee extension
  • Hamstrings — knee flexion
  • Gastrocnemius — plantar flexion

Diaphragm

The diaphragm is the principal muscle of inspiration. When it contracts, it moves downward and increases the volume of the thoracic cavity.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Muscle strain can result from overstretching.
  • Muscle atrophy means loss of muscle mass.
  • Muscle spasm is involuntary contraction.
  • Paralysis may cause loss of voluntary movement.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Points

  • Assess muscle strength.
  • Assess range of motion.
  • Prevent contractures in immobile patients.
  • Encourage safe mobility.

✍️ Hand Note

Skeletal → voluntary
Smooth → involuntary
Cardiac → heart
Diaphragm → inspiration
Biceps → flexion
Triceps → extension

❤️ Chapter 03 — Cardiovascular System

Overview

The cardiovascular system consists of the heart and blood vessels. It circulates blood throughout the body and transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste products.

🖼️ Four Chambers of Heart

Four chambers of the heart — simplified learning diagram

Location

The heart is located in the thoracic cavity within the mediastinum, between the lungs. Most of the heart lies slightly to the left of the midline.

Four Chambers

Chamber Function
Right atrium Receives systemic venous blood
Right ventricle Pumps blood to lungs
Left atrium Receives pulmonary venous blood
Left ventricle Pumps blood to body

Heart Valves

  • Tricuspid valve
  • Mitral valve
  • Pulmonary valve
  • Aortic valve
Tricuspid → right AV valve
Mitral → left AV valve

Blood Flow Through Heart

Body → venae cavae → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary valve → pulmonary arteries → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → body.

Blood Vessels

  • Arteries carry blood away from heart.
  • Veins carry blood toward heart.
  • Capillaries are major sites of exchange.

Coronary Circulation

The myocardium receives oxygenated blood from the coronary arteries. Cardiac veins drain into the coronary sinus and then the right atrium.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Myocardial infarction involves injury to cardiac muscle due to inadequate blood supply.
  • Hypertension means persistently elevated arterial blood pressure.
  • Heart failure is inability of the heart to meet circulatory demands adequately.
  • Arrhythmia refers to abnormal cardiac rhythm.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Check pulse rate and rhythm.
  • Monitor blood pressure.
  • Assess chest pain.
  • Observe edema.
  • Assess peripheral perfusion.

✍️ Hand Note

Heart = 4 chambers
Right → lungs
Left → body
Aorta = largest artery
Tricuspid = right AV valve
Mitral = left AV valve

🫁 Chapter 04 — Respiratory System

Overview

The respiratory system brings oxygen into the body and removes carbon dioxide. The respiratory tract conducts air to the lungs, while the alveoli provide the major surface for gas exchange.

🖼️ Respiratory Tract

Airway → trachea → bronchi → lungs → alveoli

Respiratory Passage

  1. Nose
  2. Nasal cavity
  3. Pharynx
  4. Larynx
  5. Trachea
  6. Bronchi
  7. Bronchioles
  8. Alveoli

Pharynx

  • Nasopharynx
  • Oropharynx
  • Laryngopharynx

Larynx

The larynx connects the pharynx with the trachea. It contains vocal folds and contributes to voice production. The epiglottis helps protect the lower airway during swallowing.

Trachea

The trachea is a conducting airway that divides into right and left main bronchi.

Lungs

Lung Lobes
Right Superior, middle, inferior
Left Superior, inferior

Alveoli

Alveoli are microscopic air sacs surrounded by pulmonary capillaries. They provide a large surface for diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide.

Pleura

  • Visceral pleura — covers lung surface.
  • Parietal pleura — lines thoracic cavity.
  • Pleural cavity — space between the layers.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Pneumonia affects lung tissue.
  • Asthma causes airway inflammation and narrowing.
  • Pneumothorax means air within pleural space.
  • Pleural effusion means excess fluid in pleural space.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Assess respiratory rate.
  • Monitor SpO₂.
  • Assess breath sounds.
  • Observe chest movement.
  • Position patient to support ventilation when appropriate.

✍️ Hand Note

Right lung = 3 lobes
Left lung = 2 lobes
Alveoli = gas exchange
Pleura = lung covering
Diaphragm = main inspiratory muscle

📝 MCQ

Answer: C. Alveoli

A. 1 lobe

🧠 Chapter 05 — Nervous System

Overview

The nervous system receives sensory information, processes information, coordinates responses and controls many activities of the body.

🖼️ Major Brain Regions

Major parts of the brain — simplified diagram

Divisions

  • Central nervous system — brain and spinal cord.
  • Peripheral nervous system — cranial and spinal nerves.

Cerebrum

The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain and is divided into two hemispheres. It is involved in conscious sensation, voluntary movement, language, memory, reasoning and many higher functions.

Lobe Important association
Frontal Motor functions, planning, aspects of speech
Parietal Somatic sensory processing
Temporal Hearing and memory-related functions
Occipital Vision

Cerebellum

The cerebellum coordinates movement and contributes to balance, posture and motor learning.

Brainstem

The brainstem consists of the midbrain, pons and medulla oblongata. It contains important pathways and centers involved in vital functions.

Spinal Cord

The spinal cord lies within the vertebral canal. It connects the brain with peripheral nerves and participates in reflex actions.

Meninges

  1. Dura mater
  2. Arachnoid mater
  3. Pia mater

Cerebrospinal Fluid

CSF surrounds the brain and spinal cord and provides mechanical protection and other supportive functions.

Autonomic Nervous System

  • Sympathetic division
  • Parasympathetic division

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Stroke affects brain function because of vascular injury.
  • Meningitis affects the meninges.
  • Spinal cord injury may produce motor and sensory deficits.
  • Epilepsy involves recurrent abnormal electrical activity in the brain.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Assess level of consciousness.
  • Assess pupil reaction.
  • Check motor power.
  • Assess sensation.
  • Monitor vital signs.

✍️ Hand Note

CNS = brain + spinal cord
Cerebellum = coordination + balance
Occipital = vision
Temporal = hearing
Meninges = dura + arachnoid + pia

📝 MCQ

🍽️ Chapter 06 — Digestive System

Overview

The digestive system receives food, mechanically and chemically processes it, absorbs nutrients and eliminates indigestible waste.

🖼️ Digestive Tract

Major parts of alimentary canal — simplified diagram

Alimentary Canal

  1. Mouth
  2. Pharynx
  3. Esophagus
  4. Stomach
  5. Small intestine
  6. Large intestine
  7. Rectum
  8. Anal canal

Mouth and Salivary Glands

Digestion begins in the mouth. Teeth break food mechanically while saliva lubricates food and begins carbohydrate digestion.

  • Parotid gland
  • Submandibular gland
  • Sublingual gland

Stomach

The stomach is a muscular organ located mainly in the upper left abdomen. It stores and mixes food and participates in digestion.

  • Cardia
  • Fundus
  • Body
  • Pyloric part

Small Intestine

  • Duodenum
  • Jejunum
  • Ileum

Most digestion and nutrient absorption occurs in the small intestine. Villi and microvilli greatly increase the absorptive surface.

Large Intestine

  • Cecum
  • Appendix
  • Ascending colon
  • Transverse colon
  • Descending colon
  • Sigmoid colon
  • Rectum
  • Anal canal

Liver

The liver is the largest internal gland and is located mainly in the right upper abdomen.

  • Produces bile.
  • Processes nutrients.
  • Stores glycogen.
  • Participates in detoxification.
  • Synthesizes many plasma proteins.

Gallbladder

The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver.

Pancreas

The pancreas has both exocrine and endocrine functions. Its exocrine part produces digestive enzymes and bicarbonate. Its endocrine part produces hormones including insulin and glucagon.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Appendicitis affects the appendix.
  • Gastritis affects gastric mucosa.
  • Peptic ulcer disease may involve stomach or duodenum.
  • Hepatitis affects the liver.
  • Gallstones may obstruct biliary flow.
  • Pancreatitis affects the pancreas.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Assess abdominal pain.
  • Monitor vomiting and dehydration.
  • Assess bowel movement.
  • Monitor nutritional status.

✍️ Hand Note

Small intestine → duodenum + jejunum + ileum
Liver → produces bile
Gallbladder → stores bile
Pancreas → digestive + endocrine functions
Large intestine → water/electrolyte absorption

🚽 Chapter 07 — Urinary System

Overview

The urinary system removes metabolic wastes and helps maintain fluid, electrolyte and acid-base balance.

🖼️ Kidney and Urinary Tract

Kidney → ureter → bladder → urethra

Main Components

  • Two kidneys
  • Two ureters
  • Urinary bladder
  • Urethra

Kidney

The kidneys are paired retroperitoneal organs located posteriorly in the abdomen. They filter blood and regulate the composition of body fluids.

Internal Structure

  • Renal cortex
  • Renal medulla
  • Renal pyramids
  • Renal papillae
  • Minor calyces
  • Major calyces
  • Renal pelvis

Nephron

The nephron is the functional unit of the kidney.

  • Glomerulus
  • Bowman's capsule
  • Proximal convoluted tubule
  • Loop of Henle
  • Distal convoluted tubule
  • Collecting duct

Functions of Kidney

  • Urine formation.
  • Waste excretion.
  • Water balance.
  • Electrolyte regulation.
  • Acid-base regulation.
  • Renin production.
  • Erythropoietin production.
  • Vitamin D activation.

Urinary Bladder

The bladder is a muscular reservoir for urine. Its muscular wall is called the detrusor muscle.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • UTI can involve different parts of urinary tract.
  • Renal calculus = kidney stone.
  • Acute kidney injury = sudden decline in kidney function.
  • Chronic kidney disease = progressive loss of kidney function.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Monitor urine output.
  • Observe urine color and characteristics.
  • Monitor fluid balance.
  • Provide appropriate catheter care.

✍️ Hand Note

Nephron = functional unit
Kidney → ureter → bladder → urethra
Glomerulus → filtration
Bladder → urine storage

📝 MCQ

B. Nephron

🧪 Chapter 08 — Endocrine System

Overview

The endocrine system consists of glands that release hormones into the bloodstream. Hormones regulate metabolism, growth, reproduction, stress response and fluid/electrolyte balance.

🖼️ Major Endocrine Glands

Major endocrine glands — simplified educational diagram

Major Glands

  • Pituitary
  • Thyroid
  • Parathyroid
  • Adrenal
  • Pancreas
  • Pineal
  • Thymus
  • Ovaries
  • Testes

Pituitary Gland

The pituitary is located at the base of the brain in the sella turcica and is connected functionally with the hypothalamus.

Thyroid Gland

The thyroid lies in the anterior neck around the trachea and produces thyroid hormones that regulate metabolic activity.

Parathyroid Glands

Parathyroid glands are usually four small glands on the posterior surface of the thyroid and regulate calcium homeostasis mainly through parathyroid hormone.

Adrenal Glands

Adrenal glands lie above the kidneys and have two major regions: cortex and medulla.

Pancreatic Islets

The endocrine pancreas contains islets of Langerhans. Insulin lowers blood glucose while glucagon raises blood glucose.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Diabetes mellitus involves abnormal glucose regulation.
  • Hypothyroidism involves insufficient thyroid hormone effect.
  • Hyperthyroidism involves excessive thyroid hormone effect.
  • Adrenal disorders can disturb cortisol or mineralocorticoid functions.

✍️ Hand Note

Pituitary → major endocrine regulator
Thyroid → metabolism
Parathyroid → calcium
Pancreas → insulin + glucagon
Adrenal → cortex + medulla

♀️ Chapter 09 — Female Reproductive System

Overview

The female reproductive system produces ova, secretes reproductive hormones and provides the organs necessary for fertilization, implantation, pregnancy and childbirth.

🖼️ Female Reproductive Anatomy

Major female reproductive organs — simplified diagram

Ovaries

The ovaries are paired female gonads. They produce ova and reproductive hormones including estrogen and progesterone.

Uterine Tube

  • Infundibulum
  • Ampulla
  • Isthmus
  • Uterine part

The ampulla is the usual site of fertilization.

Uterus

The uterus is a muscular hollow organ located in the pelvis. It provides the site for implantation and development of pregnancy.

Parts

  • Fundus
  • Body
  • Isthmus
  • Cervix

Layers

  • Perimetrium
  • Myometrium
  • Endometrium

Cervix

The cervix forms the lower part of the uterus and connects the uterine cavity with the vagina.

Vagina

The vagina is a muscular canal extending from the cervix to the external genitalia.

Mammary Glands

Mammary glands are specialized organs associated with milk production. They contain glandular tissue and ducts.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Ectopic pregnancy commonly occurs in a uterine tube.
  • Uterine fibroids are benign smooth-muscle tumors.
  • Endometriosis involves endometrial-like tissue outside the uterine cavity.
  • Cervical disease can be detected through appropriate screening.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Understand pelvic anatomy during obstetric assessment.
  • Understand uterine layers during labor and postpartum care.
  • Use anatomical knowledge for patient education.

✍️ Hand Note

Ovary → ovum + hormones
Ampulla → common fertilization site
Uterus → implantation + pregnancy
Myometrium → muscle
Endometrium → inner lining

♂️ Chapter 10 — Male Reproductive System

Overview

The male reproductive system produces sperm, secretes male reproductive hormones and transports sperm through the reproductive tract.

🖼️ Male Reproductive Anatomy

Major male reproductive structures — simplified diagram

Main Components

  • Testes
  • Epididymis
  • Vas deferens
  • Seminal vesicles
  • Prostate gland
  • Bulbourethral glands
  • Penis
  • Scrotum

Testis

The testes are male gonads located in the scrotum. They produce sperm and testosterone. Seminiferous tubules are the principal sites of sperm production.

Epididymis

The epididymis is a highly coiled tube on the posterior aspect of the testis where sperm mature and are stored.

Vas Deferens

The vas deferens transports sperm from the epididymis toward the ejaculatory ducts.

Prostate

The prostate lies inferior to the urinary bladder and surrounds part of the urethra. It contributes fluid to semen.

Scrotum

The scrotum contains the testes and helps maintain a temperature appropriate for normal sperm production.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Benign prostatic enlargement may interfere with urinary flow.
  • Prostatitis is inflammation of the prostate.
  • Testicular torsion may compromise blood supply.
  • Cryptorchidism means undescended testis.

✍️ Hand Note

Testis → sperm + testosterone
Seminiferous tubules → sperm production
Epididymis → sperm maturation
Vas deferens → sperm transport
Prostate → contributes to semen

🛡️ Chapter 11 — Lymphatic System

Overview

The lymphatic system maintains fluid balance, absorbs certain dietary fats and plays an important role in immune defense.

🖼️ Lymphatic Organs

Important lymphatic structures — simplified diagram

Main Components

  • Lymph
  • Lymphatic vessels
  • Lymph nodes
  • Spleen
  • Thymus
  • Tonsils

Lymph Nodes

Lymph nodes are small lymphoid organs along lymphatic vessels. They filter lymph and provide sites for immune-cell activity.

Spleen

The spleen lies in the left upper abdomen. It filters blood, removes aged blood cells and participates in immune responses.

Thymus

The thymus is located in the anterior mediastinum and plays an important role in T-lymphocyte development.

Major Lymphatic Ducts

  • Thoracic duct
  • Right lymphatic duct

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Lymphadenopathy = enlarged lymph nodes.
  • Lymphedema = swelling due to impaired lymphatic drainage.
  • Lymphatic spread is important in many cancers.

🧴 Chapter 13 — Integumentary System

Overview

The integumentary system includes the skin, hair, nails, sweat glands and sebaceous glands. It provides a protective barrier and participates in temperature regulation, sensation and immune defense.

🖼️ Layers of Skin

Three major levels — epidermis, dermis and subcutaneous tissue

Epidermis

The epidermis is the outer epithelial layer of skin. It provides a protective barrier and contains keratinocytes and other specialized cells.

Dermis

The dermis lies beneath the epidermis and contains blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles and glands.

Subcutaneous Tissue

Subcutaneous tissue lies beneath the dermis and contains connective and adipose tissue. It provides cushioning, insulation and energy storage.

Skin Appendages

  • Hair
  • Nails
  • Sweat glands
  • Sebaceous glands

Functions of Skin

  • Protection
  • Temperature regulation
  • Sensation
  • Reduction of water loss
  • Immune defense
  • Vitamin D-related processes

Sweat Glands

Sweat glands contribute to thermoregulation. Evaporation of sweat helps dissipate heat.

Sebaceous Glands

Sebaceous glands produce sebum, which lubricates the skin and hair.

🩺 Clinical Anatomy

  • Burns can damage skin and deeper tissues.
  • Pressure injuries result from prolonged pressure and impaired tissue perfusion.
  • Dermatitis involves skin inflammation.
  • Wounds involve disruption of tissue integrity.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Relevance

  • Inspect skin regularly.
  • Assess pressure points.
  • Maintain skin hygiene.
  • Assess wounds and burns.
  • Use pressure-injury prevention measures in at-risk patients.

✍️ Hand Note

Epidermis → outer layer
Dermis → vessels + nerves + glands + follicles
Subcutaneous → fat + connective tissue
Sweat → temperature regulation
Sebaceous → sebum

Answer: B. Epidermis

A. Digestion

Right lung = 3 lobes • Left lung = 2 lobes • Alveoli = gas exchange
🧠 Nervous:
CNS = brain + spinal cord • Cerebellum = coordination • Occipital = vision • Meninges = dura + arachnoid + pia
🍽️ Digestive:
Small intestine = duodenum + jejunum + ileum • Liver produces bile • Gallbladder stores bile
🚽 Urinary:
Nephron = functional unit • Kidney → ureter → bladder → urethra
🧪 Endocrine:
Thyroid = metabolism • Parathyroid = calcium • Insulin = lowers blood glucose
♀️ Female:
Ampulla = common fertilization site • Myometrium = muscular uterine layer
♂️ Male:
Testis = sperm + testosterone • Seminiferous tubules = sperm production
🛡️ Lymphatic:
Spleen = filters blood • Lymph nodes = filter lymph • Thymus = T-cell development
👁️ Special Senses:
Retina = photoreceptors • Cochlea = hearing • Semicircular canals = balance
🧴 Skin:
Epidermis = outer layer • Dermis = vessels/nerves/glands • Sweat = thermoregulation
